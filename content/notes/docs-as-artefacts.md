A meeting produces a feeling of agreement. A document produces an artefact: something you can point at, link to, disagree with line by line and come back to in six months. Teams that treat documents as the work, not as paperwork about the work, move faster with fewer people in the room.

## What makes a document an artefact

It has an owner, a date and a status. It says what was decided and what was rejected, and why. It is short enough to be read before anyone is asked for an opinion.

| Kind | Answers | Lives for |
|---|---|---|
| Proposal | Should we do this? | Until decided |
| Decision record | What did we pick, and what did we drop? | Years |
| Runbook | What do I do at 3 AM? | As long as the system |
| Weekly note | What changed? | A quarter |

## Rules of thumb

- Write the summary first. If you cannot, you do not know what you think yet.
- One decision per document.
- Comments are cheap, meetings are expensive: ask for comments first.
- Close the loop. A proposal without a recorded outcome is noise.

This only works when people have time to read, which is the point of [[meeting-free-fridays]]. It also pairs well with ruthless scope: see [[seksen-yuzde-kes|cut eighty percent]] (in Turkish).

## When not to write

Do not write a document to avoid a conversation. If two people are stuck, a ten minute call beats a ten comment thread. Write down what you agreed afterwards.
